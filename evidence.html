What the data shows

Peer-reviewed science and federal data, not talking points.

SAMHSA, National Survey on Drug Use and Health, 2025; Healthy Kids Colorado Survey, 2025

55% say cannabis should be legal for medical and adult use, and another 33% for medical use. Just 11% say it shouldn't be legal at all.

Pew Research Center, survey of 8,512 adults, January 2026

Myth and fact

The claims we hear most in hearing rooms, and what the evidence says.

The claim

Cannabis edibles are a top cause of child poisoning.

Rising ER visits get cited as proof of a crisis caused by legal edibles.

The evidence

Edibles don't make the top 10 for kids under 6.

Oregon Poison Center data shows household cleaners, cosmetics and pain relievers each cause more than 1,600 pediatric incidents a year. Rising ER numbers largely reflect parents feeling safer telling doctors after legalization.

Oregon Poison Center annual report

The claim

THC can stop a child's breathing, like opioids.

Stories of children on ventilators have been used to justify sweeping restrictions.

The evidence

THC does not cause respiratory depression.

THC receptors in the brainstem work differently from opioid receptors and can't directly shut down breathing. Severe cases likely involve undiagnosed conditions, extreme doses for body weight, or adulterated unregulated products.

Wiese et al., Frontiers in Neuroscience, 2023

The claim

Legalization increases teen use.

The argument that legal cannabis sends a permissive message to young people.

The evidence

Teen use keeps falling as legalization spreads.

Past-year use among 12- to 17-year-olds dropped to 8.7% in 2025, down from 10.4% a year earlier. In 2025, 74% of 12th graders and 92% of 8th graders said they hadn't used cannabis in the past year. Licensed stores check ID. Dealers don't.

SAMHSA, NSDUH, 2025; NIH, Monitoring the Future, December 2025

The claim

Dispensaries will sell to kids.

Opponents warn that legal stores put cannabis within reach of teenagers.

The evidence

Licensed stores turn minors away more often than alcohol sellers.

In Oregon's 2026 underage decoy checks through early October, cannabis shops refused 210 of 227 (about 92.5%). Alcohol sellers refused 427 of 522 (about 81.8%). Colorado reported 99% compliance across 469 checks in 2025. It isn't perfect: Oregon shops still made 17 sales, which is why enforcement matters.

Oregon Liquor and Cannabis Commission, cannabis and alcohol decoy data, 2026; Colorado Marijuana Enforcement Division, 2026

The claim

Wrapping each edible individually protects kids.

The premise behind Oregon's SB 1548.

The evidence

Safe storage is what changes outcomes.

88% of pediatric edible ER events trace to unsafe storage. The Consumer Product Safety Commission couldn't conclude that unit packaging beats standard child-resistant packaging.

OPB news report, 2025

The claim

A THC blood test proves a driver is impaired.

Several states set a fixed blood-THC limit, or zero tolerance, and treat anyone over it as a drunk driver.

The evidence

Blood THC doesn't track impairment.

In a UC San Diego study of 190 regular consumers who stopped for 48 hours, 24% were still over a 2 ng/mL limit and 43% failed zero tolerance. In a driving simulator they performed no worse than people under the limits. Never drive high. But a blood number alone can convict sober people.

Fitzgerald, Marcotte et al., Clinical Chemistry, 2025

The claim

Dispensaries make the roads more dangerous.

Opponents warn that every new store will bring more crashes to the neighborhood.

The evidence

Crash rates didn't change.

Researchers compared Connecticut before and after legalization against Maryland, and looked at crashes within half a mile of 13 new dispensaries. They found no significant change statewide or near the stores.

Weleff et al., Cannabis and Cannabinoid Research, 2025

The claim

A driver who tests positive for THC is as dangerous as a drunk driver.

Lawmakers treat any THC in a driver the same as alcohol over the legal limit.

The evidence

The crash data don't match alcohol.

In NHTSA's study of more than 9,000 drivers, testing positive for THC carried no added crash risk after adjusting for age, gender, race and alcohol (odds ratio 1.00). Drivers at 0.08 alcohol had 3.93 times the risk. Among 8,328 injured Canadian drivers, 3.3% had THC at 5 ng/mL or more, while 12.3% were at 0.08 alcohol or higher. A positive test isn't proof of impairment either way, and NHTSA warns this doesn't make driving high safe. Don't do it.

NHTSA, 2016; Brubacher et al., JAMA Network Open, 2025

The claim

Legal cannabis makes the opioid crisis worse.

Cannabis gets framed as one more addictive drug piling onto an overdose epidemic.

The evidence

Legal access is linked to fewer opioid poisonings.

In insurance claims for 107 million working-age adults, medical dispensaries were tied to a 15% drop in non-fatal opioid poisonings and adult-use laws to a 12% drop. CDC data show cannabis was listed as a cause in under 1% of overdose deaths.

Hou et al., Preventive Medicine Reports, 2026; CDC MMWR, 2026

The claim

Legalization is filling treatment centers with cannabis addiction.

Opponents point to treatment programs as proof legal cannabis is creating an addiction wave.

The evidence

Cannabis is a small and shrinking share of treatment admissions.

Cannabis was the main substance in 8.8% of the 2024 treatment admissions with a reported primary substance (115,913 of 1,319,485), and SAMHSA says that share declined gradually from 2020 to 2024. Some people do need help, and they should get it. The numbers count admissions, not people, and cover state-reported programs.

SAMHSA, Treatment Episode Data Set, 2024

The claim

Nobody gets arrested for cannabis anymore.

Lawmakers say the fight is over, so consumer protections can wait.

The evidence

Arrests rose in 2025.

Police made 218,152 cannabis arrests in 2025, up from about 203,000, and 92% were for possession. Legal states saw arrest rates fall about 85% after legalization. Prohibition states made more than eight times as many arrests as legal ones.

FBI Crime Data Explorer, 2025 data (NORML tally); Marijuana Policy Project (advocacy group) analysis, 2026

Who consumes cannabis

Not who you think. Today's consumers are veterans, nurses, retirees, parents and professionals. They vote and pay taxes.

61.6M

Americans used cannabis last year

That's 21.2% of everyone 12 and older. More than three in four of them, 47.2 million, are adults 26 and up.

SAMHSA, National Survey on Drug Use and Health, 2025

25%

of adults 30 to 49 smoke cannabis

Overall, 17% of U.S. adults say they smoke cannabis, up from 7% in 2013, and 15% use edibles. Cigarette smoking is at a record low of 11%.

Gallup, August 2026

23%

call it morally wrong

More than three in four Americans don't see cannabis use as a moral failing. The 23% who do is close to the 16% who say that about alcohol and below the 29% who say it about gambling.

Pew Research Center, 2025 survey, published 2026

How regulation works

Every licensed product goes through a chain of oversight the unregulated market never will. In order:

  1. Cultivation

    Licensed grows with state inspections, pesticide limits and documented inputs.

  2. Lab testing

    Third-party tests for potency, pesticides, heavy metals and microbes.

  3. Labeling

    Dose, ingredients, warnings and child-resistant packaging.

  4. Tracking

    Seed-to-sale systems follow every product from harvest to sale.

  5. Age check

    ID at the counter. 21 and over only.

Licensed store

  • Lab-tested for potency and contaminants
  • Child-resistant packaging required by law
  • Accurate dose on every label
  • ID checked, 21 and over
  • State inspections and licenses
  • Every product traceable
  • Recourse if a product is mislabeled

Unregulated market

  • Unknown potency, pesticides or additives
  • Often sold in unmarked containers
  • No dose information
  • No age check
  • No inspections
  • No way to trace a bad product
  • Synthetic cannabinoids increasingly common

Know your rights

Your rights vary by state. These are the basics so you're never caught off guard.

Growing at home

Many legal states let adults grow a few plants for personal use. Oregon allows up to 4 per household for adults 21 and over. Know your state's limit and keep plants out of public view.

Driving and travel

In legal states you can usually carry cannabis in a sealed container in your car. Open containers are treated like open alcohol. Never cross state lines with cannabis, even between two legal states.

At work

Some states bar employers from punishing off-duty use. Many don't, and safety-sensitive jobs and federal contractors have fewer protections. Check your state's law before telling an employer. Keep it off the clock: a study of 2,745 Canadian workers found that use away from work didn't raise injury risk on the job, but use before or during work nearly doubled it (Institute for Work & Health).

Medical patients

Patients often get higher possession limits, access where adult use isn't legal, and sometimes job and housing protections.

If you're cited or arrested

Stay calm. Don't argue at the scene. Don't consent to a search without a warrant. Contact a lawyer who knows cannabis law. NORML's legal committee can help.

Your right to speak up

You can testify at hearings, write your legislators and vote. When consumers show up, outcomes change: Oregon's 10mg edible cap passed the Senate in 2026, then died in the House after consumers and businesses pushed back.

State-by-state laws: NORML. This is general information, not legal advice.

Policy watch

Bills that affect consumers in Oregon, Hawai'i and nationally. For ballot measures this November, see Rollback Watch.

  1. 2026Oregon Legislature
    Defeated

    SB 1548: edible packaging and 10mg cap

    Would have required individual wrapping for every edible piece regardless of dose and capped each piece at 10mg THC. The claims behind it weren't supported by Oregon's Poison Center data, peer-reviewed pharmacology or packaging research. It passed the Senate 22-5, then died in a House committee after consumers and businesses pushed back.

    Sen. Reynolds has said she'll bring back a 10mg cap with new warning labels in 2027. Oregon today caps packages at 100mg with no per-piece limit.

    Marijuana Moment, March 2026; August 2026

  2. FederalDEA
    Active

    Rescheduling to Schedule III

    An April 2026 Justice Department order moved state-licensed medical cannabis and FDA-approved products to Schedule III. The DEA hearing on rescheduling all cannabis was paused on September 29 so a GAO report on gaps in the scheduling process could be considered. Lawsuits from opponents are consolidated in the D.C. Circuit, which has refused to block the change. Adult-use cannabis is still Schedule I, and criminal penalties haven't changed.

    Marijuana Moment, September 2026

  3. Dec 11, 2026Congress
    Takes effect

    Federal ban on hemp-derived THC

    Products with more than 0.4mg total THC per container will no longer count as legal hemp under federal law. A stopgap funding bill signed September 3 pushed the start date from November 12 to December 11. Synthetic cannabinoids the plant can't produce naturally are still banned starting November 12. Bills to regulate instead of ban are pending, but none has passed.

    Marijuana Moment, September 2026; Akerman LLP

  4. Hawai'iLegislature
    Watching

    Adult-use legalization

    Hawai'i keeps delaying adult-use policy. Consumers need to organize before the next session.

Poison Control

Save the number. If a child eats cannabis, call right away. Most cases are manageable with quick attention.

Lock it up

Use a locked box or cabinet. A high shelf isn't enough.

Keep the original package

Licensed products come in child-resistant packaging. Don't move them into unlabeled containers.

Talk to your household

Everyone at home should know where cannabis is kept, why it's locked, and that it isn't food even if it looks like it.

Research library

The sources behind our arguments: peer-reviewed science, government data and established polling, grouped by public health and youth, consumer safety, medical use, workplace and driving, criminal justice, equity, markets and taxes, and polling. Newest first within each topic.

  • Public health & youth

    Minor decoy operations, cannabis retailers

    Oregon Liquor and Cannabis Commission open data, 2026

    In 2026 checks through early October, licensed cannabis shops refused an underage decoy 210 times out of 227 (about 92.5%). Alcohol sellers refused 427 of 522 (about 81.8%, alcohol data). Cannabis shops still made 17 sales, so enforcement matters.

  • Public health & youth

    Cannabis detection among overdose deaths, 2021 to 2025

    CDC, Morbidity and Mortality Weekly Report, 2026

    Across 209,166 overdose deaths in 31 states and DC, cannabis was detected in 21% but listed as a cause in 0.8%. It was the only drug involved in 9 deaths.

    “Cannabis was listed as causing death in 0.8% of overdose deaths.”
  • Public health & youth

    Cannabis use among veterans and non-veterans

    Do, Rustagi et al., Journal of General Internal Medicine, August 2026

    In federal survey data on 907,136 adults from 2016 to 2023, 9.9% of veterans and 13.0% of non-veterans currently used cannabis. Young veterans used far less than civilians their age (11.8% versus 23.6% at 18 to 24), while veterans 50 and older had higher odds of using than non-veterans the same age.

    “Overall, older Veterans were more likely to report cannabis use, used cannabis more frequently, and were more likely to inhale cannabis than their non-Veteran peers.”
  • Public health & youth

    National Survey on Drug Use and Health, detailed tables

    SAMHSA, July 2026 (2025 data)

    Past-year use among 12- to 17-year-olds fell to 8.7% in 2025 from 10.4% in 2024. Across all ages 12 and up, 61.6 million people (21.2%) used cannabis in the past year.

  • Public health & youth

    Prenatal cannabis exposure and child development: 72 studies reviewed

    Domingues, Fonseca and Sá, European Child & Adolescent Psychiatry, July 2026

    Most of the 72 observational studies found no adjusted link between cannabis use in pregnancy and children's overall cognition, language or motor skills. But most did find links to weaker behavior regulation (73% of studies), attention (69%) and executive function (70%), sometimes stronger with heavier use. The safest choice in pregnancy is not to use.

    “Current evidence does not consistently support global neurodevelopmental impairment following prenatal cannabis exposure but suggests a pattern of selective vulnerability in behavioural, attention and executive regulatory domains.”
  • Public health & youth

    Drug-Free Communities Support Program

    U.S. Government Accountability Office, June 2026

    GAO found the White House drug office lacks the performance data to back its claim that this federal youth prevention program reduces substance use.

    “However, that report states that it is not possible to establish a causal relationship between substance use changes in communities and the DFC program.”
  • Public health & youth

    Cannabis use before and after legalization in Germany

    Kotz et al., International Journal of Drug Policy, 2026

    In a sample of 35,957 people, Germany's 2024 reform was not associated with any change in past-year use or heavy use two years in.

  • Public health & youth

    Treatment Episode Data Set, 2024

    SAMHSA, 2026

    States reported 1,583,823 treatment admissions in 2024. Among the 1,319,485 with a reported primary substance, cannabis was the main one in 115,913, or 8.8%, and SAMHSA says its share declined gradually from 2020 to 2024. The data count admissions, not people, and cover state-reported programs.

    “Marijuana/Hashish gradually represented a smaller percentage of admissions over the 5-year period…”
  • Public health & youth

    Healthy Kids Colorado Survey, 2025 results

    Colorado Department of Public Health and Environment, 2026

    Current cannabis use among high schoolers fell from 13% in 2023 to 10% in 2025, and the share who say it would be easy to get dropped from 40% to 34%.

    “10% of youth used marijuana within the past month”
  • Public health & youth

    Cannabis use and metabolic disease in the UK Biobank

    Zhou et al., Nutrition, Metabolism & Cardiovascular Diseases, April 2026

    Among 91,002 UK adults followed over time, cannabis users had an 18% lower risk of type 2 diabetes (hazard ratio 0.82), and heavy users a lower risk of obesity. The pattern reversed for moderate users who already had a BMI over 30. It's observational, so it can't show cannabis caused the difference.

    “Cannabis use was associated with modestly lower observed risks of several metabolic outcomes, particularly among individuals with BMI<25.”
  • Public health & youth

    Youth cannabis use in Minnesota, 2025

    Minnesota Department of Health, December 2025

    Two years after Minnesota legalized, 95.9% of 8th, 9th and 11th graders reported no cannabis use in the past month. Use has declined every survey since 2013.

    “In 2025, 4.1% of Minnesota students reported any past month cannabis use, while 6.3% reported any past year use.”
  • Public health & youth

    Use of sleep aids among adults, 2024

    CDC National Center for Health Statistics, 2026

    3.7% of adults used cannabis or CBD most days to help them sleep, compared with 5.2% using prescription drugs and 5.7% using over-the-counter aids.

    “Overall, 5.2% of adults used prescription medications, 5.7% used OTC medications or supplements, and 3.7% used marijuana or CBD products as sleep aids.”
  • Public health & youth

    Cannabis-related hospitalizations among children and youth in Manitoba

    Taillieu, Sareen et al., International Journal of Population Data Science, July 2026

    Ten years of data on residents 18 and under found no statistically significant rise in cannabis-related hospitalizations after Canada legalized.

    “…legalization of cannabis did not appear to increase the number of cannabis-related hospitalizations among children and youth in Manitoba…”
  • Public health & youth

    Cannabis use among middle-aged and older adults

    Livne, Hasin et al., American Journal of Preventive Medicine, March 2026

    In the national Health and Retirement Study (1,324 respondents), 18.5% of adults 50 to 64 and 5.9% of those 65 and older used cannabis in the past year. About a quarter and a fifth of those users, respectively, used it medically, and more than 75% in both groups said medical use is acceptable.

    “…past-year cannabis use in the U.S. was reported by 18.5% and 5.9% of middle-age and older adults, respectively…”
  • Public health & youth

    2025 Regulatory and Enforcement Update

    Colorado Marijuana Enforcement Division, February 2026

    Colorado ran 469 underage compliance checks at licensed cannabis businesses in 2025 and reported a 99% compliance rate. Regulators also investigated 309 licenses as part of preventing youth access.

    “The Colorado Marijuana Enforcement Division (MED) conducted 469 underage compliance checks in 2025 as part of its focus on preventing youth access to marijuana…”
  • Public health & youth

    Cognition the morning after cannabis use

    Matheson, Di Ciano et al., Drug and Alcohol Dependence Reports, 2026

    Regular consumers tested 12 to 15 hours after smoking showed no significant differences from 65 non-users on any cognitive test. Higher-potency products were linked to weaker memory scores.

  • Public health & youth

    Drug harms in Canada: an expert ranking

    Crépault et al., Journal of Psychopharmacology, January 2026

    Twenty experts scored 16 substances on 16 kinds of harm to users and others. Alcohol scored highest at 79, then tobacco at 45 and non-prescription opioids at 33. Cannabis ranked sixth at 15, just behind cocaine and methamphetamine at 19. Scores reflect harm across the whole population, which depends partly on how many people use each drug.

  • Public health & youth

    Monitoring the Future, 2025

    National Institutes of Health, December 2025

    Survey of 23,726 students at 270 schools. Past-year cannabis use was about 26% among 12th graders, 16% of 10th graders and 8% of 8th graders.

    “Cannabis use remained stable among all grades, with 8% of eighth graders, 16% of 10th graders, and 26% of 12th graders reporting use in the past 12 months.”
  • Public health & youth

    Motor vehicle crashes near dispensaries after legalization

    Weleff, Mathis et al., Cannabis and Cannabinoid Research, 2025

    Comparing Connecticut with Maryland, legalization had no significant effect on crashes statewide, and crash rates within 800 meters of 13 new dispensaries didn't change.

  • Public health & youth

    Cannabis poisoning in Oregon

    OPB (news report), August 2025

    Reporting on Oregon's pediatric exposure data, including the Poison Center director's view that storage education is what works.

  • Public health & youth

    California legalization and drinking among Kaiser patients

    Van Doren et al., Addiction, July 2025

    Across 8,028,627 primary-care screenings of 3,525,493 Kaiser Permanente Northern California patients from 2015 to 2019, the 2016 legalization vote was followed by gradual declines in drinking and heavy drinking, most consistently among adults 35 to 49. Some younger and older groups saw short-term increases first.

  • Public health & youth

    Oregon Poison Center Annual Report

    Oregon Health & Science University

    Cannabis edibles aren't among the top 10 causes of accidental poisoning in Oregon children under 6. Cleaners (1,659), cosmetics (1,636) and pain relievers (1,606) rank far higher.

  • Public health & youth

    Cannabis hospitalizations among Canadian youth after legalization

    Myran et al., American Journal of Public Health, May 2025

    Across 137,901 cannabis-related hospitalizations of Canadians 15 to 44, hospitalizations for youths under the legal age fell 34% relative to those over it three and a half years after legalization, while hospitalizations of legal-age adults kept rising. A published letter argues the authors overstate the finding.

  • Public health & youth

    How adults use cannabis

    CDC, Morbidity and Mortality Weekly Report, April 2025

    In 2022 survey data on 138,625 adults in 22 states and two territories, 15.3% used cannabis in the past month. Of those, 79.4% smoked, 41.6% ate edibles, 30.3% vaped and 14.6% dabbed, and 46.7% used more than one way. It isn't a national sample.

    “In 2022, 15.3% of adults reported current cannabis use, approximately 80% of whom reported smoking.”
  • Public health & youth

    Adult-use laws and adult drinking and smoking

    De and Sun, American Journal of Preventive Medicine, February 2025

    Among 4,841,891 adults surveyed from 2012 to 2022, passing an adult-use law wasn't tied to any change in drinking or tobacco use. Once stores opened, current drinking fell 0.95 percentage points and cigarette smoking 0.48 points, though some groups used more smokeless tobacco. The authors call the overall effects limited.

    “Considering the passage of laws as cannabis law implementation, recreational cannabis laws were not associated with any alcohol or tobacco use outcomes in the fully adjusted model.”
  • Public health & youth

    High school marijuana use, 2011 to 2021

    Yang et al., Pediatric Reports, October 2024

    In CDC survey data on 88,183 high schoolers, current use fell from 23.1% in 2011 to 15.8% in 2021, and trying it before age 13 fell from 8.1% to 4.9%, over a decade when many states legalized. It's a national trend, not a test of legalization itself.

    “…dropped significantly from 23.1% in 2011 to 15.8% in 2021…”
  • Public health & youth

    Current cannabis use among U.S. youth and adults, 2013 to 2022

    Mattingly, Richardson and Hart, Drug and Alcohol Dependence Reports, September 2024

    In federal survey data on 543,195 people, past-month use among 12- to 17-year-olds held steady from 2013 to 2019 and again from 2021 to 2022, while overall use among everyone 12 and older rose from 7.59% to 11.48% and from 13.13% to 15.11%. A 2020 survey change splits the trend into two periods.

  • Public health & youth

    The Endocannabinoid System and Breathing

    Wiese et al., Frontiers in Neuroscience, April 2023

    University of Arizona pharmacology review. States plainly that THC doesn't cause respiratory depression, and separates natural THC from far more potent synthetic cannabinoids.

  • Public health & youth

    Retailer marketing and compliance in five western cities

    Berg et al., Addictive Behaviors, 2023

    Audits of 150 randomly chosen stores in Denver, Seattle, Portland, Las Vegas and Los Angeles in 2022 found age-verification rates above 90%. Auditors also flagged problems: 20.7% had youth-oriented signs, 18.0% youth-oriented packaging and 28.7% made health claims.

  • Public health & youth

    Pediatric Cannabis Edible Exposures: California ED Survey

    Western Journal of Emergency Medicine, 2021

    45% of cannabis users in homes with children got no storage information. Only 44.8% kept cannabis both locked and hidden.

  • Consumer safety

    CBD label accuracy across 937 products

    Szyrner, Serracino-Inglott and Vella Szijj, University of Malta, Phytochemistry Letters, June 2026

    Pooling 28 lab studies from 2017 to 2025, only 31.3% of 937 CBD products had CBD within 10% of the label. Topicals were worst at 13.8% and oils best at 41.3%. Some contained THC, synthetic cannabinoids, heavy metals, pesticides or solvents, which is the case for testing and labeling rules, not bans.

  • Consumer safety

    CBD and liver enzymes in advanced cancer

    Scarborough et al., BMJ Supportive & Palliative Care, 2026

    Pooling two Australian placebo-controlled trials in 287 people with advanced cancer, CBD at up to 600 mg a day and a THC/CBD product didn't raise liver enzymes over four weeks, and no treated patient crossed the safety limits. These are short-term results only.

    “Medicinal cannabis did not cause elevation of ALT or AST in patients with advanced malignancy at the doses studied (up to 600 mg CBD/day).”
  • Medical use

    Cannabis laws and opioid prescribing for veterans in pain

    Mannes, Hasin et al., Journal of General Internal Medicine, September 2026

    In VA health records for chronic-pain patients from 2013 to 2022, medical cannabis laws were linked to a 0.79-percentage-point drop in opioid prescriptions of 30 days or more and a 0.34-point drop in long-term opioid therapy, with small increases in high-dose prescribing. Adult-use laws were tied to further declines. The authors call the effects modest.

    “Findings suggest that greater legal cannabis access may be associated with modest reductions in opioid prescriptions among patients with chronic pain…”
  • Medical use

    Oral THC for PTSD nightmares: a randomized trial

    Roepke et al., Nature Medicine, August 2026 (Bionorica-funded trial)

    In a 10-week German trial of 171 people with PTSD, a bedtime dose of oral THC (dronabinol, 2.5 to 15 mg) eased nightmares more than placebo, a moderate effect. Side effects were more common on THC (89.7% versus 77.1%), and serious adverse events occurred in 8.0% on THC versus none on placebo. The drug's maker funded the study.

    “This trial provides evidence that dronabinol reduces the frequency and intensity of PTSD-related nightmares, although long-term efficacy and safety require further evaluation.”
  • Medical use

    Cannabis legalization and non-fatal opioid poisonings

    Hou, Talbert and Jayawardhana, Preventive Medicine Reports, 2026

    Using insurance claims for 107 million adults aged 18 to 64, medical cannabis dispensaries (MCDs) were tied to a 15.5% drop in non-fatal opioid poisonings and recreational cannabis laws (RCLs) to an 11.9% drop.

  • Medical use

    Cannabis legalization and opioid use among people who inject drugs

    Haley, Cooper et al., Drug and Alcohol Dependence, March 2026

    Among 28,069 people who inject drugs (PWID) in 13 states, living where adult use is legal (versus medical only) was linked to a 9 to 11% lower chance of daily opioid misuse.

  • Medical use

    Medical cannabis and cancer symptoms

    Minnesota Office of Cannabis Management, September 2026

    Of 6,621 cancer patients who bought medical cannabis, among those with moderate to severe symptoms, 47% saw vomiting improve by at least 30% within four months, and about 40% for nausea and for anxiety. 14.5% reported side effects, mostly mild.

    “…47.2% (n = 845) reported a ≥30% reduction in vomiting scores within four months of first medical cannabis purchase.”
  • Medical use

    Benefits of medical cannabis for patients with pain

    Christensen, Park and Rak, Clinical Therapeutics, September 2026 (Minnesota program data)

    Among 6,010 chronic pain patients with moderate to severe symptoms, about 55% gained at least 30% in life enjoyment and 41% saw that much pain relief at four months.

    “…54.9% reported a ≥30% score improvement for life enjoyment, 54.7% reported improvement in general activity interference, and 40.8% reported improvement in pain score…”
  • Medical use

    Cannabinoids and sleep: a meta-analysis of trials

    da Silva et al., Sleep Medicine Reviews, December 2025

    Across six randomized trials with 1,077 patients, cannabinoids improved self-reported sleep quality over placebo. Products with THC or other non-CBD cannabinoids showed larger effects, while CBD-only products showed no significant benefit. Results varied widely between trials.

  • Workplace & driving

    Impairing substances among injured drivers in Canada

    Brubacher et al., JAMA Network Open, April 2025

    Blood tests on 8,328 injured drivers at 15 trauma centres from 2019 to 2023 found 16.3% had any detectable THC and 3.3% had 5 ng/mL or more, compared with 12.3% at a blood alcohol level of 0.08% or higher. Detecting a substance doesn't prove impairment.

  • Workplace & driving

    Workers' cannabis use and injury risk in Canada

    Carnide et al., Institute for Work & Health, Canadian Journal of Public Health, 2023

    Following 2,745 Canadian workers from 2018 to 2020, using cannabis away from work wasn't linked to a higher risk of injury on the job. Using it before or at work nearly doubled the risk.

    “Compared to no past-year cannabis use, there was no difference in workplace injury risk for non-workplace cannabis use (RR 1.09, 95%CI 0.83–1.44).”
  • Workplace & driving

    Recreational legalization and workers' compensation

    Abouk, Ghimire, Maclean and Powell, National Bureau of Economic Research working paper, 2021

    Among adults 40 to 62, adult-use (recreational marijuana, or RML) laws were followed by a 0.18-percentage-point drop in receiving workers' compensation (WC), about 20% of a low baseline, and a 20.5% drop in annual benefits. It's a working paper, not yet peer reviewed.

    “We find that WC receipt declines in response to RML adoption both in terms of the propensity to receive benefits and benefit amount.”
  • Workplace & driving

    Drug and alcohol crash risk: a case-control study

    National Highway Traffic Safety Administration, December 2016

    Comparing more than 3,000 crash-involved drivers with more than 6,000 others in Virginia Beach, THC-positive drivers had no higher crash risk once age, gender, race and alcohol were accounted for (odds ratio 1.00). Drivers at 0.08 breath alcohol had 3.93 times the risk. NHTSA cautions that this doesn't make driving high safe.

    “This study should not be interpreted to mean that it is safe for individuals who have used substances to operate a vehicle – this is a complex issue.”
  • Criminal justice & arrests

    Racial disparities in California cannabis arrests, 2025

    California NORML (advocacy group) analysis of California DOJ arrest data, September 2026

    California made 2,432 marijuana arrests in 2025. Adjusting for population, Black residents were arrested at 6.4 times the white rate, up from 5.2 in 2024 and 3.51 in 2016 before legalization; Latinos at 2.26 times. Arrests have collapsed but the gap has widened, though small numbers make the ratios swing year to year.

    “Blacks were 6.4 times more likely as Whites to be arrested for marijuana crimes…”
  • Criminal justice & arrests

    Police made 218,152 cannabis arrests in 2025, up from about 203,000 in 2024. 200,960 were for possession, roughly 23% of all drug arrests, and about 13% of agencies didn't report.

  • Criminal justice & arrests

    Legalization, arrests and racial disparities

    Meinhofer, Rubli and Cunningham, American Economic Journal: Economic Policy, May 2026

    Legalization cut possession arrests 62% for white people and 51% for Black people across 11 states, narrowing the racial gap but not closing it. Serious violent and property crime did not rise, though hospitalizations involving cannabis and other drugs did.

    “We find that legalization reduces cannabis possession and sales arrests for White and Black populations, narrowing but not eliminating disparities.”
  • Criminal justice & arrests

    Recreational marijuana legalization and personal bankruptcy

    Wang and Wu, Finance Research Letters, 2026

    Adult-use laws cut cannabis arrests by about 87% and were linked to roughly 12% fewer personal bankruptcy filings, as fewer households faced fines and legal fees.

  • Criminal justice & arrests

    Per se DUI laws and blood THC after abstinence

    Fitzgerald, Marcotte et al., Clinical Chemistry, 2025

    After 48 hours without cannabis, 24% of 190 regular consumers were over a 2 ng/mL legal limit, yet they drove a simulator no worse than people below it.

    “Many regular users of cannabis exceed zero tolerance and per se THC cutpoint concentrations days after their last use, risking legal consequences despite no evidence of impairment.”

    How taxes affect prices and legal cannabis demand

    Yoon, Shang et al., International Journal of Drug Policy, 2026

    Across adult-use states from 2014 to 2023, a 10% tax increase cut legal sales 12.6%. Each $1 of tax raised retail prices $4 to $6, and higher taxes lowered revenue.
