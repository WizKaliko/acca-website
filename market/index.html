Who controls the market?

A small group of multi-state operators holds most of the stores where licenses are capped, puts its brands on thousands of shelves, and funds the campaigns, trade groups and super PACs that shape who gets to compete. This is what the public record shows.

      The alphabet soup.

      Over a decade, the biggest operators built a chain of trade groups, coalitions and PACs, each one feeding the next. The names change; many of the companies and people stay the same.

      1. 2010National Cannabis Industry Association (NCIA)

        The first national trade group, open to businesses of every size, with dues of $1,000 to $15,000 a year. Its revenue peaked at $4.0M in 2019 and was $0.74M in 2025.

      2. 2018Cannabis Trade Federation

        Formed in April by merging the New Federalism Fund with the American Trade Association for Cannabis and Hemp, the same year two Colorado operators' leaders left NCIA's board. Its founding board companies included Green Thumb and PalliaTech, then Curaleaf's parent. It spent nearly $1M on federal lobbying in 2019.

      3. 2019National Cannabis Roundtable

        Launched with former House Speaker John Boehner, then on Acreage's board, as honorary chair and Acreage's CEO as chair. Cresco's Charlie Bachtell became chair in 2020, with Trulieve's Kim Rivers as second vice chair. About $695K in lobbying its first year.

      4. 2021U.S. Cannabis Council

        Founded by the Trade Federation, the Marijuana Policy Project and ten-plus MSOs. The Federation granted it $331,775. Its platform paired legalization with equity and repair. In 2022 an equity group on its board quit, criticizing its corporate focus.

      5. 2023A super PAC and a scheduling coalition

        The Council launched the "Legalize America" super PAC, chaired by a Curaleaf executive, later renamed American Rights and Reform PAC. Seven MSOs joined the Coalition for Cannabis Scheduling Reform.

      6. 2025US Cannabis Roundtable

        The Roundtable and the Council merged in January. Bachtell is chair and Rivers vice chair. The groups' PAC raised $6.79M, 96% from five MSOs, and gave to Trump-aligned committees.

      7. 2026America First Agriculture Action

        Six MSOs gave $11.5M in June to a new super PAC that shares a treasurer with MAGA Inc.

      Governors, attorneys general and legislative groups

      Governors and attorneys general enforce and shape licensing. These national groups elect them, and they report donors to the IRS, not the FEC.

      Who gets in first, and how many get in at all.

      License caps, head starts for existing medical operators and limits on new license types decide how much competition you get. Here is where MSOs showed up on those questions.

      • 2019Illinois

        Existing medical operators got the first adult-use licenses.

        Cresco's CEO was the only cannabis operator on Gov. Pritzker's legalization transition subcommittee. That August, Cresco's president told investors the company had been "involved in the crafting of that legislation."

        Cresco Labs press release, May 31, 2019; Q2 2019 earnings call, Aug. 21, 2019

      • 2020Arizona

        Prop 207 gave existing dispensaries the first window to sell.

        Harvest Health gave $1.42M by late October 2020, with Curaleaf and MedMen also funding the campaign. By October 2023, just 4 of the 26 original social-equity winners still had a stake in their licenses.

        Arizona campaign finance reports via press; Arizona Center for Investigative Reporting, Oct. 2023

      • 2023New York

        MSOs sued to stop the equity-first licensing program.

        A coalition including Curaleaf, Green Thumb, Acreage and PharmaCann challenged New York's CAURD program in court. The settlement let medical operators open adult-use stores starting in December 2023.

        N.Y. Supreme Court, Albany County, Index No. 902390-23

      • 2023Maryland

        Curaleaf and Trulieve asked to keep a four-store limit, not two.

        The bill proposed cutting how many dispensaries one company could own from four to two. Both companies testified to keep four. The final law kept four.

        Written testimony on HB 556 / SB 516, Feb. 2023

      • 2024Florida

        Amendment 3: incumbents first, no home grow.

        Trulieve funded nearly all of the campaign. The text let the existing licensees sell to adults and left new licenses to the Legislature. It won 56% but needed 60%.

        Florida Division of Elections; initiative text

      • 2024–25Congress

        MSOs lobbied to close the hemp THC "loophole."

        Green Thumb, Verano and Jushi reported federal lobbying on hemp-derived THC, which competes with licensed dispensaries in about half the country.

        Lobbying Disclosure Act filings, 2024–2025

      • 2025Ohio

        Verano was the only MSO to testify on SB 56.

        It opposed new potency caps and cuts to grower canopy. It did not address the bill's 400-dispensary cap or the repeal of Ohio's social-equity program.

        Ohio Senate committee testimony, Feb. 2025

      Equity on the label. Not in the law.

      Every one of these companies talks about equity. Look at what they actually put into it, and where it came from, next to what the same companies did to the rules that decide who gets a license.

      Our view: the giving is real, but it is small, often required by law or paid by customers, and it goes to causes that don't touch market structure. The money that shapes who can compete runs the other way.

      Prisoners, yes. Licenses, no.

      The best-funded cause in this table is the Last Prisoner Project, which works to free people imprisoned for cannabis and clear their records. It's vital work. But LPP says equity licensing is outside its focus, so it doesn't push on caps, head starts or consolidation. From 2019 to 2022, Harvest, Cresco, Ascend, Green Thumb, Trulieve and AYR all publicized donations or fundraising for LPP. Over the same years, the Minority Cannabis Business Association lobbied Congress and testified that MSOs get head starts and exemptions from license caps, and we found little public record of MSO funding for it.

      How we did this

      We matched 1,155 subsidiary and license-holding companies and 222 executives and directors from SEC filings against federal and state campaign-finance records, IRS political-organization filings, federal lobbying reports, nonprofit tax returns, legislative testimony and earnings calls. Individuals appear only if they are an officer, director or founder of one of these companies. Press figures were replaced with filed amounts wherever filings exist, and headline claims were checked against a second source.

      What this doesn't cover yet: state giving covers seven states; Arizona and Virginia records weren't searchable online. Trade-group dues are not public. Store counts come from state lists where they exist, and brand reach from an industry data panel, not every store.
